Bring what's due. Leave sure of it.

Margin lives inside a larger learning app, and it is more than a box for doubts. A student opens it with something due: tonight's homework, a textbook exercise, Friday's test. Margin gets her through it in her book's way, teaches her on the way, and remembers, so that tomorrow is easier and she comes back without being pushed.

The short answers

Why do they come?

To get unstuck on one question that is due. Then to understand it, then to get ready for a test. In India, 73% of children who use AI use it for doubts and practice, and most of that is homework.

What should it feel like?

Useful in the first minute, from her own book, never a dead end, and always one clear next step. It should feel like the favourite teacher who also knows her week.

How do they find what it can do?

Inside the reply first, as her turn and a few chips; then one “next for you” card; the library only when pulled up; the home screen when there is no question.

What brings them back?

The school week, not a guilt streak: what is due, what is fading, what sits underneath and what is coming. Daily use grows when the school, teacher and parent are in.

Why they come

Nobody opens a study app for AI. They open it because something is due and they are stuck.

Surveys of Indian and American students agree on the order. The first reason is one specific question, usually homework or a textbook exercise. Understanding comes second, getting ready for a test third. Underneath all of them is a feeling: no one nearby can help, and asking in class feels risky.

  1. 1

    Get unstuck on this question, tonight

    Homework, a textbook exercise, a question from class. In India, 73% of children who use AI use it for “doubt solving and practice”, and the survey says that includes finishing homework. Maths is the top subject. In the US, 43% of teens use chatbots to solve maths problems.

    73%of Indian children using AI, for doubts and practice

  2. 2

    Understand it, in words that make sense

    The top reason Indian children keep using AI is “easy-to-understand explanations” (45%), then quick answers (41%), “feels like chatting with a person” (40%) and being able to ask a follow-up (35%).

    45%keep using AI for its explanations

  3. 3

    Be ready for the test

    32% of Indian children who use AI use it to prepare for tests; among users of learning apps it is 47%. In Class 10, the board exam sets the whole year.

    47%of learning-app users, for test preparation

  4. 4

    Check that I got it right

    Google says 75% of Photomath users check their own answers with it; the claim is undated. Before a test this is the main job: “is my working right, and where would I lose marks?”

    75%Photomath's own claim, undated

  5. 5

    Look something up, or follow a curiosity

    48% of US teens research topics with AI; 25% of Indian children who use AI ask out of curiosity. This is a smaller reason to open a study app, and the general assistants serve it well.

    25%of Indian AI users, for curiosity

Indian figures from the Bharat Survey for EdTech 2025 (Central Square Foundation; 12,500 children from low-income homes in ten states, fieldwork July 2025 to January 2026). US figures from Pew Research, 24 February 2026. The groups differ, so the percentages are not a single ranking; the order is the same in every survey found.

When

In the evening, after school and tuition, and just before a deadline. In the only hourly data found (a US university course), 47% of help requests came between 7 pm and midnight. Use rises and falls with the school year and peaks before exams.

Where they go now

YouTube (94%), WhatsApp (67%) and Google (49%). Only 6% use a dedicated learning app. And 63% started using any learning technology because a school or teacher recommended it.

How they feel

28% of 3.79 lakh students say they hesitate to ask questions in class. A Class 10 student said: “There is a fear of asking doubts to teachers. But AI feels like a friend and helps us.” 87% get help at home, mostly from mothers, who can often supervise but not explain the maths.

So the first thing Margin must do well is take the exact question in front of her and get her through it, kindly and fast.

Explaining, checking, review and plans are all earned by doing that first job better than YouTube, Google Lens and ChatGPT. And because most children start using learning tools when a school or teacher recommends them, the school is not an extra. It is a door.

The student

A CBSE student loves a teacher who gets them through the exam, on the phone they already hold.

Their real study tools today are YouTube teachers, Google Lens and a parent's phone in the evening. What they value is tied to marks. What they and their parents reject is anything that blocks tonight's homework, shows ads or pushes a sale.

Their favourite teacher, on YouTube

143.5M subscribers across PhysicsWallah's 379 YouTube channels

Students subscribe widely but watch only the chapter they need: Magnet Brains has 14.6 million subscribers and averages about 9,300 views a video. A Class 10 maths one-shot by Shobhit Nirwan has 11 million views.

For Margin: sound like a favourite teacher, not a search engine. Offer the two minutes of video that answer the doubt, and a full revision video only when asked.

Multibagg, 17 Nov 2026 · Qoruz, Nov 2026

Point the phone at the problem

61% of urban 10 to 16 year olds who use AI use it for homework

54% of urban children aged 10 to 16 use AI tools, and 67% of them say they turn to AI because their parents are too busy. Google says India uses Lens more each month than any other country, with education a top use.

For Margin: the camera is the front door. Typing a maths question on a phone is the obstacle.

vivo and CMR, Dec 2025

Marks, and being ready for the test

81% of 3.79 lakh students named studies and exams as their main worry

Sample papers, previous years' questions, the marking scheme and “important questions” are what they search for. 27% of students take private coaching, 38% at secondary level. Asked what maths help they want, 54% of urban parents said “more regular practice”.

For Margin: connect every question to the test: how marks are given, a similar past-paper question, “ready for Friday”.

NCERT survey, 2022 data · government education survey, 2025 · The Week, 23 Oct 2026

Hinglish, and a teacher who talks like them

13% of low-income children learning with technology say content is not in their language

The large free channels keep separate Hindi-medium and English-medium channels. Urban Hindi speakers often type to AI in Hinglish, and models follow instructions less well in mixed Hindi and English.

For Margin: accept Hinglish, reply in the language of the question, keep the written maths in the form the exam expects.

JMIR, 2026

Games, streaks and being seen

77.4M monthly players of Free Fire in India

In Peru, a streak feature raised both use and maths scores among 60,000 children in Grades 4 to 6. In India, a reading contest with a leaderboard raised stories read by about 45%, and the effect partly lasted after prizes ended.

For Margin: streaks that count learning and can be repaired; recognition from the teacher and the parent; word problems in worlds they like, such as cricket.

IPR working paper, Feb 2026 · arXiv 2310.10850 · TechCrunch, 28 Oct 2026

A phone that is often a parent's

54% of urban parents limit their child's screen time

About two thirds of 57,000 urban parents call their child addicted to social media, video or games. Yet only 26% of Indians want AI banned in schools, against 78% who want social media banned for under-14s.

For Margin: the parent is allowed to like this app. Be light on data, resume where she stopped, and leave the parent a short, honest note.

LocalCircles, Dec 2025 · Ipsos, 31 Aug 2026

What puts them off

  • Hints that never end. In a 2026 Tennessee trial, students ignored or argued with Khanmigo when it only gave hints.
  • A cap in the middle of homework. “Doubts Exhausted” after two questions, as we saw on Infinity Learn.
  • Ads and sales calls. Indian parents still distrust education apps after the refund and mis-selling complaints of 2021 and 2022.
  • Being made to type a maths question with fractions and symbols on a phone keyboard.
  • Long lectures when they wanted one line, though they choose two-hour revision videos the night before an exam.

Why students pick a particular YouTube teacher has no Indian survey behind it; it rests on how the channels describe themselves. No nationwide survey of AI use by 9 to 16 year olds exists yet. Full notes and sources: research_notes/Product experience 2026-11-25/what-cbse-students-love.md.

Cues from those who came before

Earlier startups show what fails. The best products show what keeps people.

Each lesson below changed something in Margin's experience. The startup lessons come from what happened to the products, not only to their finances.

DoubtnutValued near $150M, sold for about $10M

Snap a photo, get a video solution, free. It built huge reach, but the answer was all it gave. Nothing brought a student back except the next doubt, and ChatGPT then gave the same answer for free.

For Margin: the answer gets her in. What happens after it, and on the days with no doubt, is the product.

Finshots, Dec 2023

CheggStaff cut by about 45% in Oct 2025

A paid bank of answers. When free assistants and AI answers in search gave answers too, the reason to pay was gone.

For Margin: an answer alone is free everywhere. Charge, if ever, for what is hers: her book, her week, her progress.

Company filings; see the 17 November market notes

BYJU'S“71 minutes a day” claimed; about 11 a month reported

Sold to parents with urgency and fear, often on loans, with refunds that did not come. Children did not use what was bought. Engagement claims could not be checked.

For Margin: earn the child's daily use first; show the parent only what they can check; never sell to the parent through fear.

The Ken, citing App Annie (secondhand); parent complaints, 2021 to 2022

Snapchat My AI75% one-star reviews in a week

Pinned at the top of every user's chats, with no way to remove it.

For Margin: inside the larger app, Margin is offered where it helps, never forced onto screens where it is not wanted.

Duolingo64.9M daily users, up 29% in a year

A lesson before sign-up raised daily users about 20%. A short daily unit anyone can finish. Streaks that can be repaired. Hundreds of small tests, each worth one to four per cent.

For Margin: value before any set-up; a two-minute daily unit; a streak that forgives; test everything in small steps.

What the chat products have learnt

The question of what an AI tutor should feel like is the same one ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude face.

The blank box fails

An empty chat box gives no clue what to ask. Broad example prompts help; narrow ones and early tips are ignored.

NN/g, Mar 2024

Follow-ups under the answer work best

Of all prompt suggestions, the ones placed under an answer are the most useful. Generic ones are ignored.

NN/g, Apr 2025

Modes a user must find go unused

Study modes sit behind a switch. OpenAI has published no figure on how many students turn it on.

General assistants notes, 16 Nov 2026

Reaching out needs a reason

ChatGPT's proactive daily brief, Pulse, launched in September 2025 and was retired in June 2026, replaced by tasks users set themselves.

OpenAI Help

Few people press thumbs-down

Explicit feedback came on under 1% of interactions in one study. Errors must be found by checking, not by waiting for complaints.

arXiv 2509.18361

Locking answers away backfires

Students who could use AI freely did better than those made to read first (0.21 SD, 334 students). University students mostly ask for answers and skip about half of the hints.

IZA, Dec 2025 · arXiv 2604.23486

The Margin experience, defined

The favourite teacher who also knows her week.

A good teacher answers the question you brought, the way the class does it, checks you can do the next one, notices what you keep getting wrong, and reminds you before the test. Margin should feel like that, on the phone, at 9 pm, every day. Eight rules make that true; each comes from the evidence above.

  1. 1

    Start from her work, never a blank box

    The camera, her chapter's questions and what is due are on the first screen.

    Why: the question is on paper; an empty chat box gives no clue what to ask; most children come with a doubt.

  2. 2

    Useful in the first minute

    No sign-up, no tour, no setting to choose. The larger app already knows her class and book.

    Why: moving sign-up after the first lesson raised Duolingo's daily users by about 20%.

  3. 3

    Her book, her teacher's way

    The NCERT method, the page and the picture, prepared and checked by subject experts. With school notes, her teacher's steps.

    Why: her teacher marks the notebook. A right answer in another method is a wrong answer to her.

  4. 4

    Get her through tonight, and teach on the way

    Margin chooses between the answer and guided steps. She can always reach the full working. A parent or teacher can lock homework to steps.

    Why: students stop using tutors that only hint (Khanmigo in Tennessee); free access beat “read first” in a 334-student trial.

  5. 5

    Show one next thing, not everything

    Her turn and two or three chips in the reply; one “next for you” card; the library only when pulled up.

    Why: follow-ups under the answer are the suggestions people use; generic ones and walls of links are ignored.

  6. 6

    A reason to come back, every day

    Due, fading, underneath, coming: the home screen is her week. A streak counts learning and forgives a missed day.

    Why: repairable streaks and short daily units built Duolingo's habit; ChatGPT's proactive brief without a real reason was retired.

  7. 7

    Never a dead end

    Every failure has its own way out: fix what I read, the other method, smaller steps, a mentor. No doubt limit in the middle of homework.

    Why: under 1% of people press thumbs-down; PhysicsWallah puts a person behind its AI.

  8. 8

    Honest with the family

    A weekly page of what she can now do, the same for her and her parent, including when an answer was shown instead of taught.

    Why: the parent owns the phone and pays; BYJU'S showed what selling on fear does to trust.

Count

“I read” comes first. Photo solvers that show what they read let the student fix a misread before it spoils the answer. It is the cheapest trust a reply can earn.

The reply is the book's working, kept short: the page, one picture, the chain of equal fractions the book uses, and one sentence of meaning. This is the four-part reply from the earlier page, unchanged.

It ends with her turn. A question she answers with a tap, then three chips that are the other ways Margin can help with this same question.

Speed is part of the experience. In the 18 November test Margin took 10.9 seconds to its first words on this question. A first reply that feels slower than Google Lens loses a child on day one. Target: first words in under three seconds, with vetted answers served straight from the bank.

Four layers, each showing a little more.

  1. 1
    Inside the reply: a question for her and chips for the other kinds of help. This is where most students will find most features.
  2. 2
    Right after it: one “Next for you” card, chosen from how she answered. One choice, not fifteen.
  3. 3
    Pulled up when wanted: “More on this” holds videos, labs, games, practice and mix-ups. It is a bottom sheet on a phone and a side panel on a laptop. It is the shelf, not the front door.
  4. 4
    On the home screen: what to do next, when there is no question in hand (Moment 5).

New things are shown once, when they fit. The first time she types out her own working, Margin offers: “Snap your notebook next time and I'll find the line that went wrong.” A feature met at the moment it would have helped is remembered; a feature in a tour is not.

Every kind of failure has its own way out.

  • Read the photo wrong“I read” with Edit, before any answer.
  • Not how my teacher did itShow the book's other method; remember her choice for the chapter. With school notes uploaded, use the teacher's own method.
  • Still stuck after the explanationDrop to smaller steps and ask her to fill in one part, then offer a person: the app's mentor.
  • A wrong answer from MarginOne tap to flag it; a flagged vetted answer goes back to the subject experts.
  • Out of free doubtsNever mid-homework. Infinity Learn's “Doubts Exhausted” screen in our test is the opposite of this.

The home screen is the reason to open it on a day with no doubt. At most four cards, each saying why it is there:

  • What is due, from a diary photo, or from the school when it is connected.
  • What is fading: spaced review, always a new question, on the right day.

The streak counts days she made something sure, a review answered or a doubt closed with her own correct answer. It does not count minutes or app opens. It sits small in the corner, and missing a day does not reset it to zero.

What brings her back

Five loops bring her back. The school's week is the strongest.

A study app cannot count on a doubt every day. It needs reasons that recur on their own. Each loop below starts from something real in her life, so a reminder is a help, not a nag.

Homework every evening, a class tomorrow, a test on Friday. The trigger comes from school, so Margin only has to be the easiest place to act on it.

Should she spend more time in it?

More minutes is the wrong goal. BYJU'S advertised 71 minutes a day while a tracker reportedly saw about 11 a month, and the gap destroyed trust. A child who spends an hour stuck in Margin has had a bad evening.

The right goal is more of her study going through Margin: homework, review, test plans, and the app's videos and games reached from a reply. Time grows as a result, and every minute is one a parent is glad of.

Reminders, with rules

At most one a day, only for something real (due, fading, a test), never in quiet hours the parent sets. No guilt wording, and no message about a broken streak.

Why: Duolingo's own tests found the right reminder adds a few per cent of retention, while a 2025 review of children's apps flagged its “excessive notifications”. For children, the parent decides whether the app stays.

Where Margin is different

Her book, her teacher's way

The NCERT method and page, checked by subject experts; with school notes, the teacher's own steps. The general assistants answer well, but not from her book.

Her week

Homework, timetable and test dates turn into what to do tonight. No product found plans from a school's week.

Her history

Doubts, checks and marks feed spaced review and gap repair, across classes, with the parent's consent.

The app around it

Videos, labs, games, practice and mentors are already in the app. Margin is the way in to all of them, one step at a time.
